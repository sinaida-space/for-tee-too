# Creative-ideation library

Adapted from the author's own guide "От «что же мне сделать?» до вау-продукта"
(sinaida.eu). Translated and condensed for session use — pull one entry at a time,
never the whole file, into the conversation.

## Blockers (use only when she flags "stuck")

Each blocker has a trigger pattern and one matching unblock move. Identify the
pattern from how she describes being stuck, then offer the single matching move as
a next-step choice — don't lecture on the neuroscience unless she asks.

1. **Blank page paralysis** — too many possibilities, no way to start despite having
   what's needed. Move: pick one hard constraint right now (one color, 15 minutes,
   one paragraph) and start from the middle, not the beginning.
2. **Illusion of understanding** — solving the wrong problem, usually discovered
   too late. Move: state the problem in three versions — for herself, for the
   client, for a stranger — and see where they diverge.
3. **Anchored on the first good idea** — first working solution becomes the only
   one; everything else gets judged against it, not on its own. Move: ban the
   current idea for 24 hours and look for others without it, or force 7 more
   principally different directions.
4. **Perfectionism as blocker** — standard so high that starting feels impossible.
   Move: name the current version a draft explicitly ("v0.1"), set a hard time
   deadline, define "good enough" as a written checklist before starting.
5. **Reference overload** — too many references kill her own voice. Move:
   quarantine references for the first two hours of work; extract "why it works"
   as one principle per reference instead of copying form.
6. **Premature inner critic** — evaluation kills ideas before they're formed.
   Move: 10 minutes of continuous generation with no deleting, or ask her to name
   the critic and tell it to wait outside.

## The 9 thinking tools

Mode key: ✎ pencil/paper works best · ○ LLM speeds it up · ✎+○ both work.

1. **5 Whys** ✎ — ask "why" five times against the symptom, each answer becomes
   the next question, written not spoken. Use when: she thinks she knows the cause
   but solutions keep not working.
2. **Six Hats (de Bono)** ✎+○ — six modes in sequence, one at a time: white
   (facts), red (emotions), black (risks), yellow (upside), green (ideas), blue
   (process). Use when: stuck evaluating from one angle only, or a group can't
   agree.
3. **Reverse brainstorm** ✎ — instead of "how to solve it," ask "how to guarantee
   it fails," list failures, then flip each one into a solution. Use when: normal
   brainstorming produces only obvious, boring ideas.
4. **SCAMPER** ○ — Substitute / Combine / Adapt / Modify / Put to other use /
   Eliminate / Reverse, applied to one object or concept. Use when: there's a
   prototype or base idea and it needs variations.
5. **Mind map** ✎ — center idea, first-level branches, sub-branches, drawn by
   hand for speed over order. Use when: she needs to dump everything she already
   knows before starting.
6. **Constraint design** ○ — add artificial constraints (one color, no words, ten
   minutes, zero budget, for a blind user) — constraint as generator, not brake.
   Use when: too much freedom is paralyzing, or a genuinely unusual angle is
   needed.
7. **Analogical thinking** ○ — find how an analogous problem is solved elsewhere
   (nature/biomimicry, military strategy, cooking, architecture, theater); port
   the underlying principle, not the form. Use when: a fresh image or metaphor is
   needed, especially for visual/art work.
8. **Jobs to be Done** ○ — not "what are we building" but "what job is the person
   hiring this to do," across three levels: functional, emotional, social. Use
   when: working on a product, a piece of communication, or a visual language for
   an audience.
9. **Prototyping loop** ✎+○ — build the roughest working version in 30-60 minutes,
   show 2-3 real people with no explanation, note what reads on its own, iterate.
   Quality comes from cycles, not more deliberation.

## Symptom → method matrix

| She says... | Reach for |
|---|---|
| I don't know what the actual problem is | 5 Whys |
| My ideas are obvious and boring | Reverse brainstorm |
| I have no ideas at all (visual/art) | Mind map + analogies |
| I have an idea, I need variations (product/UX) | SCAMPER |
| A team/collaborator can't agree | Six Hats |
| Too much freedom, nothing sticks | Constraint design |
| I don't understand the audience (product/UX) | Jobs to be Done |
| I need an unusual image or metaphor (visual/art) | Analogical thinking |
| I can't tell if this idea actually works | Prototyping loop |

## Pencil vs. LLM

- Prefer pencil/paper for: 5 Whys (keeps the thought from drifting), reverse
  brainstorm (sticky notes, spatial thinking through movement), mind maps (speed
  over the mouse).
- Prefer LLM for: SCAMPER (20+ variations fast), constraint design (50 options,
  keep 3), analogical thinking (breadth of domains), Jobs to be Done (structured
  three-level analysis).
- Both: Six Hats (paper for the emotional/red-hat pass, LLM to structure the
  synthesis), Prototyping loop (build by hand, but the LLM can role-play the test
  audience's first reaction).

## AI-as-coauthor protocols (how *you*, the assistant, should behave — not to explain to her unless she asks)

1. **Mirror, not author** — don't hand over a finished answer to insert unedited;
   surface one resonant element and let her write the final version herself.
2. **Socratic mode** — when she wants to think something through, default to
   questions only, no solutions, until she's stuck on one — that's the real work.
3. **Black-box protocol** — before offering anything, have her state her current
   position first; compare before/after rather than replacing her view outright.
4. **Voice iteration** — if a generated line "sounds right but isn't hers," ask
   her what's off, revise for that disagreement, not for "better."
5. **Ownership test** — can she defend the final call with three of her own
   reasons and zero reference to "the AI said so"? If not, it isn't finished.
