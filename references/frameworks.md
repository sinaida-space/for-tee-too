# Business-case and scoping question banks

Condensed from her 12-point scoping list, plus a few additional frameworks worth
folding in depending on project type. Pull one question at a time — never dump the
list.

## Business case (Phase 3 source material)

1. **Problem** — what's not working, and what makes her say that? Inefficiencies,
   limitations, accessibility issues, cost, other pain.
2. **Business case** — why does it matter (to her, to the business, to whoever's
   affected)? What breaks if it doesn't happen?
3. **Intro** — why is she doing it, how, what exactly is "it"?
4. **Before / after** — concrete contrast: how things work now vs. how they'd work
   with this in place.
5. **Who benefits** — clients/audience, and specifically how their life gets
   easier or better.
6. **Goal / CTQs** — what does success look like, concretely and measurably?
7. **Scope** — in scope / out of scope for this round.
8. **Competitors / comparable work** — who else is doing something like this, and
   what's genuinely different here? (Skip gracefully if truly none apply.)
9. **What's been tried already** — customer discovery, prior attempts, relevant
   numbers, conclusions so far. Guide's explicit warning: don't let a project stay
   at "just an idea" — ask what's actually been tested, not only imagined.
10. **Team / expertise** — who's making this real, and why will they pull it off?
11. **Resources** — what already exists vs. what's still needed.
12. **Risks and mitigations** — delivery risk and operational risk.
13. **Market potential** — current value in this niche, what share is realistic to
    aim for. Skip depth for pure personal art unless she raises it herself.
14. **Business model** — how budgeting works, how it's priced/sold, how revenue
    or sustainability actually happens (commissions, grants, licensing, exposure,
    sales — whatever genuinely fits).

## Additional frameworks (draw from these when they fit — don't announce the
## framework name unless she'd find it useful; just ask the underlying question)

- **Premortem (Gary Klein)** — for the risk question: "imagine this failed badly
  six months from now — what happened?" Surfaces sharper risks than a raw list.
- **Working Backwards / PR-FAQ (Amazon)** — for goal framing on product-shaped
  ideas: "write the headline for when this launches successfully — what does it
  say?"
- **Value Proposition Canvas** — for the "who benefits" question on client/product
  work: what's their job-to-be-done, their pains, their gains, and does this
  actually address them?
- **Lean Canvas / Business Model Canvas** — structure for the business-model
  question when the project is venture-shaped: channels, cost structure, revenue
  streams, unfair advantage.
- **Kano model** — for scoping: which features are basic expectations, which are
  the ones that create delight, when scope needs trimming.
- **Double Diamond (Design Council)** — useful framing for the overall arc
  (diverge on the problem, converge on it, diverge on solutions, converge on one)
  if she wants to see how Phases 1-4 map onto a named model.
- **Ansoff Matrix** — if market potential involves deciding between new/existing
  audience vs. new/existing offering.
- **Ikigai** — for personal or art projects specifically: does this sit at the
  overlap of what she's good at, what she loves, what the work is for, and what
  it can sustain? Useful for the "why this, why now" gut check, not as a rigid
  grid.

## Ownership test (run in Phase 6)

Ask, without offering an answer:

1. "Say the final direction back to me in your own words — no reference to
   anything I said."
2. "Give me three reasons this is right, that don't cite 'the AI suggested it.'"
3. If she can't produce three — that's the signal the decision isn't fully hers
   yet. Loop back into whichever phase feels thinnest rather than finalizing.
